{\LARGE\bfseries Exploiting Opponents in Drop the Handkerchief}\par
James Cenawood \hfill September 29, 2026
\vspace{4pt}

In the registered v1 study, translated Hal wins \textbf{93.9\%} against player emulators, where exact Hal wins 54.7\%.
A guard caps Hal's expected loss against \textbf{any} opponent, but no guarded Hal beat translated Hal in a registered test.

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.55\textwidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\head{1. From solving to exploiting}
Exact Hal plays the certified equilibrium of Drop the Handkerchief (DTH) [1].
No opponent does better than the game value against it, but it gains
nothing from a player's habits. We build Hal policies that forecast the
opponent from the public history and answer that forecast in the exact
stage game.

At state $x$, the tablebase supplies the continuation-adjusted matrix
$M(x)$ and the value $W(x)$ [1, Section 4]. In Hal's view, let $H=M$
when Hal drops and $H=-M^{\mathsf T}$ when Hal checks, so rows are
Hal's seconds and entries are Hal's values; $W_H$ is the value in the
same view. Given a forecast $q$ of the opponent's second, Hal plays a
hard best response to $Hq$, with equal mass on tied seconds. The policies below
differ in how they form $q$ and in the matrix they answer.

\head{2. Perfect Mode Hal}
Perfect Mode (PM) Hal fuses forecast sources for each opponent role:
the uniform policy, the opponent's equilibrium, a decayed Dirichlet
count, Perfect Hal v1's expert mixture, change-point detection [5], an
outcome model, and an optional recurrent network. Fixed Share [4]
learns their weights. Four modes move from the equilibrium to hard
forecast responses, and we measure each chosen policy's local
worst-case loss against a fresh matrix.

We registered a 56-identity confirmation in commit \texttt{b76147f}.
PM scored .8705, behind Perfect Hal v1 at .9554, and failed its
promotion gate (Table 1). Its record
\texttt{pm\_hal\_confirmation\_v3.json} sits in \texttt{src/arena/config}.
Later builds replaced the tablebase manifest that it binds, so we do not
rerun v3.
PM plays local pure-DTH games; the hosted game does not offer it.

\head{3. Translated Hal}
Translated Hal adds eight offset experts to Perfect Hal v1 (Old). Each
copies or mirrors one of Hal's recent seconds with a learned offset.
It beat Old by 2.11 points $[1.55,2.66]$ over 8,192 synthetic games and
tied Old against emulators of 8 recorded players. A selector network
that reweights its 24 experts added 1.52 points $[0.49,2.59]$
(Table 2). We did not register either study in Git before it ran, so
we treat both as development evidence.

\head{4. Exploit continuation}
$M$ assumes equilibrium play after the current half-round. Hal keeps
exploiting, so $M$ undervalues the states where Hal survives. We replace
the value $W(y)$ of each live child $y$ by the value of Hal's best
response to a child forecast $q'$, in the child Dropper's view:
\[
v(y)=\begin{cases}\max_d\,(M(y)\,q')_d,&\text{Hal drops at }y,\\
\min_c\,(q'^{\mathsf T}M(y))_c,&\text{Hal checks at }y.\end{cases}
\]
We rebuild the look-ahead matrix $L$ from these values with the
transition classes of [1, Section 4]. At each child, a best response
earns at least the maximin value, which lies within $10^{-6}$ of $W(y)$.
Thus $L\ge H$ entry by entry in Hal's view, up to that tolerance. The
\emph{same} forecast reuses $q$ at each child; the \emph{role} forecast
asks the model for the role the opponent takes there. A decision
rebuilds up to 61 child matrices in about 25\,ms.

\head{5. A guard on realized gifts}
$L$ trusts $q'$ at every child, so an opponent who predicts Hal can
punish it. Following Ganzfried and Sandholm [3], a guard gives each game
a budget $k_0=\epsilon_0$. Hal plays its best response to $Lq$ when
$\min_b(\pi^{\mathsf T}H)_b\ge W_H-\max(k,0)$; otherwise a linear program
finds the best policy that meets this floor, with the equilibrium as a
fallback. After the opponent reveals $b$, the budget absorbs the gift
\[
g=\pi^{\mathsf T}He_b-W_H,\qquad k\leftarrow k+g.
\]
\textbf{Bound.} In pure DTH, a guarded Hal has
$\mathbb E[G]\ge W_H(s_0)-\epsilon_0-\mathbb E[S]$ against any opponent,
where $G=\pm1$ is the outcome and $S$ sums the turns' floor misses.

\textbf{Proof.} Each transition raises the progress score $\Phi$
[1, Section 3], so play ends at a terminal state with $W_H=G$, and
\[
G-W_H(s_0)=\textstyle\sum_t\big(W_H(s_{t+1})-W_H(s_t)\big).
\]
The opponent commits $b_t$ without seeing Hal's second, and Hal draws its
second from $\pi_t$, so term $t$ has conditional mean $g_t$. The floor
gives $g_t\ge-\max(k_t,0)-m_t$, so $k_{t+1}\ge\min(k_t,0)-m_t$ and
$\sum_tg_t=k_T-\epsilon_0\ge-\epsilon_0-S$ on every path. \textup{QED}

The certificate can place the maximin of $H$ up to $10^{-6}$ below $W_H$,
so a turn can miss the floor by $m_t$. Confirmation measured misses of
at most $9.8\times10^{-10}$. Leap turns use the equilibrium and leave $k$
unchanged, so the bound covers pure DTH.

\head{6. A registered test}
We score a finished pure-DTH game by
\[
\hat s=\tfrac12\Big(1+W_H(s_0)+\textstyle\sum_t\big(\pi_t^{\mathsf T}H_t\mu_t-W_H(s_t)\big)\Big),
\]
where $\mu_t$ is the simulated opponent's mixed policy. The same
telescoping makes $\hat s$ an unbiased estimate of the score. We average
one-step action and revival outcomes; sampled paths contribute variance.

We pushed the runner with a frozen protocol in commit \texttt{b8f87b4}
before any game. Sessions play four games from 8:12 in paired seats
against four opponent blocks: 19 league families, emulators of 8
recorded players, translated Hal as the opponent, and a counter. The
counter copies the controller from the public history and answers its
exact mixed policy. Selection kept the arms that match translated Hal on
the league and stay within .025 of it against the Hal model, then picked
the best on the emulators. Confirmation used fresh seeds, and promotion
needed every gate in Table 3.

\head{7. Results}
In canonical STL [2] against the emulators, translated Hal scored .939
(11,543--745) and exact Hal .547 (6,718--5,570). The player-clustered
difference is $+.393\,[.370,.407]$.

The open arms gained 3 to 4 points on the emulators but fell to
.34--.40 against the Hal model (Figure 1). Guards held the Hal model
near .5, and against the counter each guarded arm reached its bound
$.5-\epsilon_0/2$. One arm passed selection: the role forecast with
$\epsilon_0=.25$. In confirmation it gained 3.5 points on the league
and tied translated Hal on the emulators, but it failed three gates.
We therefore deploy translated Hal.

The bound held (P4): over 6,976 games the least gift sum was
$-.2500000001$. The hosted runtime check passed (P6) with a decision
p95 of 24.9\,ms.

Against its counter, translated Hal lost all 512 confirmation games.
Its public code lets a player compute Hal's mixed policies. The emulators
fit 98 games against exact Hal and contain no reaction to exploitation.
The v1 self-repeat fit used observed repeat rates as copy weights, which
increased repetition. We retain these historical results; the corrected
v2 fit needs a new study. A human win-rate claim needs a live randomized comparison.
